For Khaled and Shav

2021

Meir Shalit Perhaps I reside somewhere in between. Meir Shalit continued walking, for what had surely been hours, on feet that stepped over dust and stones and broken pathways. He wore old, fraying, brown shoes, and was not particular in his choice to do so. Today was no different than the day before it, or the day before that. Every day, as if in a trance, Meir Shalit put on his old soldier shoes that had remained intact—apart from two seam-tares and paper-thin soles (that allowed him to feel everything he stepped on)—for ten and some years. Sometimes, Meir got the strange sensation that his shoes were more of a body to him than were his blood, flesh, skin, and bones. He knew that, truly, he could live as this old pair of shoes just as he could live as a walking body—this was about the only thing Meir was certain of. Another knock. "I am searching for my friend, Shav. Do you know of him? Do you know where his body lies?" Do you know where his body lies? Meir had spent the entire morning asking this question, again, again, and again, as if trapped in a sort of rhythm. And each time, he'd receive a similar response—a pair of eyes widening, then sharpening (and then oftentimes closing), a slur of angry words, a door slamming shut. He closed his eyes, now, and remembered the purpose of his wandering. "Bring him to me, Meir. Bring my son home to me. My only son, my Shav. Don't let him stay alone, cold and bare on the dirt…don't let him rot…don't let his flesh leave his bones…Bring him home, Meir! My son, my only son." A mother's plea. Shav, Meir's friend since childhood, had been killed in November, during a Fedayeen attack on the Jordanian border of Southern Hebron. "Leave him, Meir! We've got to go—now! He's done for, it's no use…no use." Meir still heard these words often, as if they were ingrained in his natural pattern of thought—and they were knives to him, penetrating each of his ear canals, slowly…meeting somewhere in the middle of his brain, touching, point to point. "I will bring him home. I will bring Shav home to you." Meir knew, though—albeit somewhere outside of the realm of his conscious awareness—that his search was in vain. And Shav's mother must have known as well. It had been months. Months. Shav no longer had a body that enclosed him to call his own. He was dirt, nothing more. Yet Meir continued to knock. In his mindless searching, he came upon the village of Al-Samu. He stood before a small house and thought about its ugliness—windows were cracked (and had been replaced with thin sheets, light yellow and light blue), white wooden panels were missing, and an entire section of roofing was gone. Compared to others, though, it wasn't all that bad. It was still standing. Another knock. A young girl (Meir guessed about fifteen or so), opened the door. She was dark—darker than he—and had thick, brown hair that curled loosely at the ends. It was not neatly brushed—messy, even, as a child's head of hair often is. And she had big, sharp, toffee-brown eyes that oddly numbed Meir—but they didn't widen, sharpen, or close. Meir swallowed dry air. "I am searching for my friend, Shav. Do you know of him? Do you know where his body lies?" Jameena Alaba'd Before she opened her eyes, Jameena Alaba'd felt her mother caressing her hair—twirling strands of it, braiding others, with fingers that danced to a soothing melody. Jameena was in that state of unrepressed subconscious, in which her mind was touched by both dreams and reality. But slowly, her mother's fingers stopped dancing. Jameena awoke with still-fogged vision, alone, in her bedroom. Her mother had not stroked or braided her hair in months. Nor had she softly sung as she used to, every morning. Jameena's mother essentially stopped all movement—recoiled into an empty body, that hung to life by only a thread—after the death of Khaled Alaba'd, her only son and Jameena's older brother. On November 13th, 1966, the day that Khaled was killed, he turned 19 years old. Jameena could remember that day, vividly. Khaled had just returned home two days earlier, from service—she didn't know exactly of his whereabouts but remembered that he had mentioned crossing the border into Israel. And on the morning of the day of his death, Khaled had smiled—Jameena remembered the moment, and would, forever. In his final years, Khaled seemed to have grown hardened to life, perhaps because of his duties (surely overwhelming for just a boy), or perhaps because of his learned detachment from the world around him. Jameena remembered that her brother—the know-it-all, the bravest of sorts, who was over-confident and playfully rude, and who was just in the beginning stages of budding—had, after joining the Fedayeen, begun to endlessly ask questions. Everything confused Khaled—it was as if his mind were degenerating…no longer able to piece together simple sequences of information as a mind is meant to. What is a sky? Jameena, what is a sky? Sometimes, when he asked his questions, Khaled revealed wild eyes that were full of fear. Other times, though, they were empty. Do you know who I am? My hands, my feet, hair, skin, eyes…do you know where I am, Jameena? Most times, she didn't answer her brother. But on that day—the day Khaled had smiled and perished—Jameena had answered. And it was her response that made Khaled smile. Mother had prepared knafeh, Khaled's favorite dessert, for his birthday. When he walked into the kitchen that morning, and found Jameena beside the dish his mother had made, he asked her: "Mother is letting us eat this so early in the day? Sweets before sustenance?" He teasingly reversed his mother's oft-spoken rule, tilting his head just slightly to the side in an act of confusion. "Why?" Jameena played along. "Well, today is nothing special, Khaled. Perhaps she's gone mad. She must have gone mad." And at that, he smiled. Knafeh was the last food that Khaled tasted. It must have still been sweet on his tongue when he was shot twice in his back, and then, after falling to the ground, once more through his left temporal lobe. Now, as Jameena slowly awoke and rubbed her eyes, granting them more and more sight, she wondered what she would prepare herself for breakfast. Father was out looking for work (as was his daily routine, after losing employment a few years back). Mother was in bed still, surely, savoring moments of peace that only her unconscious mind could offer. Jameena rose, and stretched her arms above her head, arching them back and turning her head to each side, extending her neck and shaking off sleep's last remnants. She walked towards the kitchen but was interrupted by a knocking on the front door. … Meir received no response. The young girl stood at the door, looking slightly baffled, and arched her head forward, as if searching. A curl of hair fell over her right shoulder. She wore a thin-looking, sleeveless, white sleep-dress, that ended just below her knees, and she stood bare-foot. Meir thought he saw her right big toe curling forward, pressing hard into the wooden floor, perhaps in confusion, or perhaps in fear. He knew it was useless, but he repeated his plea: "I am searching for my friend, Shav. Do you know of him? Do you know where his body lies?" Jameena curiously searched the man standing before her, in her doorway. He was older than her, probably by around ten years. He was tall, but not strong—a little frail, even. He wore a distressed green soldier's uniform, with pocketed pants and all sorts of zippers. He is one of them. The man's nostrils widened and then shrunk, and they did this again and again, through pouts of exhausted breath. He removed his hat now, and held it in both hands, folding it a bit in an obvious effort to occupy himself. But his head didn't drop, and his eyes retained that familiar, bewildered stare. A child's eyes. Jameena often took careful note of the sets of eyes she encountered—unless closed, they whispered to her all kinds of secrets, against the will of their possessor. As she looked into the eyes before her, Jameena thought—rather, she was certain—that she'd seen them before. Do you know where his body lies? "No…No, I don't. I know nothing at all." She hesitated. "But, please, come inside. Maybe I can help." … The two now sat at Jameena's kitchen table. Meir felt odd—too large—in his seat, as his knees pressed against the under-layer of the small slab table. He placed his hat on his lap. Jameena, empty-stomached, ripped a piece of zaatar bread for herself from the counter—leaving only a small piece remaining. But she took this piece and held it out in an offering-gesture to the man sitting across from her at the table. Meir accepted, and shoved the piece of bread, whole, into his mouth, remembering how hungry he was. "Your name?" She answered despite a full mouth of bread. "I'm Jameena. Jameena Alaba'd." She swallowed. "And you?" Meir told her his name, and the two finished their bread. "This man you are searching for—Shav—who is he? Was he a soldier, like yourself?" "He was, yes." The corner of Meir's mouth curved up as he thought about the weight, and the undeniable humor, of the question he'd been asked. Who is Shav? He wanted to laugh. Who was Shav? Was he the young boy who, in grade school, stuck his tongue out at their first-grade teacher, and, after receiving punishment for doing so, panted Meir in front of the whole class just to further feed the flame? Or was he the prepubescent teen who—to deceive his overly-strict father—had his first kiss with a girl on Meir's front doorstep, rather than his own? Was he the grand food-enthusiast, who always asked his mother to teach him how to cook his favorite dishes, gathering a book of recipes that someday, he'd hoped to prepare as head chef in his own restaurant? Or, was he the scared kid whose face was covered in dirt, tears, and sweat, as he lay writhing on the ground, deaf from the sounds of gunshots, crying for home and his mother, and holding his bloody entrails in his hands? "He was killed in November. A Fedayeen attack." It was all Meir could manage. And then: "And his mother would like me to bring him home." Jameena did not respond. What could she say? This stranger doesn't know true loss. "You…you must know one of them, or even some of them? They're all around here—based in the area, no? Those filth-mongering maggots. Dirty, rotten things…If I found one now, even off-duty—" "I do! That's right, I do! My very own brother—Khaled—is one of them, and do you know what he told me?" Jameena didn't exactly pause giving him a window to do so, but in that moment Meir instinctively arched forward, not knowing what to expect. "He told me that he killed your Shav. He told me that he shot him down, shot him to bits." Shot him down, shot him down, shot him down…She repeated her slurs until hot tears covered her cheeks. And Meir sat there, dumbstruck, staring at the young girl who was a broken machine before him. He would have stood, acted as a man does in war, let his rage take him and beat down this broken machine, had it not been for Jameena's tears. This young girl did not know about Shav. She didn't know how he lived or how he died. To her, Shav was everything and nothing. It didn't matter either way. Meir remained sitting. "Khaled—your brother. Where is he now?" He spoke softly, suddenly all too aware. Jameena didn't want to answer. How could this man—this stranger—come into her home and ask her about Khaled? But she willed herself to respond. "November took him too." Meir extended his hand, reaching towards the girl who mourned the loss of her brother, and then letting it drop and rest, palm upwards, on the table between them. "رحمه الله." He'd overheard it so many times before. Jameena's eyes closed, and she nodded in acceptance. "Khaled was a soldier? "Yes." She nodded again. "But on the day he was killed, he wasn't a soldier. He was only Khaled." "Where did—" Meir started to ask. "Here. He was home. Here in Al-Samu." Jameena went on. "I only remember pieces of the day. Khaled and I sat in the kitchen eating knafeh—we sat here actually, here at this table. You are sitting in Khaled's seat." Meir shifted, not out of discomfort, but out of a certain longing to know this Khaled. "And then?" "He sat right there," Jameena pointed to Meir's seat again, "when the gunshots first rang." Meir closed his eyes, could hear them, even. He stared at his still-open palm that was facing the ceiling and thought about every time it had pulled a trigger in live-fire. It was strange, though. Here, lying upward on this kitchen table, it shed all remnants of strength, and looked lifeless. Dead. "When he heard them, Khaled didn't even hesitate. He didn't speak either. He only looked at me—pleadingly—and then stood up, and ran through the front door, towards the commotion. I still don't know why, but he didn't bring anything with him—not his gun, not his coat. It was cold." "I ran after him, shouting all the while. He must've heard me…he must've known that I was following him, because when he passed our street's corner—just a few feet ahead of me—he suddenly turned around. 'Jameena, go home! Go home!'" Home. Meir heard Khaled's cries as if he were standing only a short distance behind the boy, just as Jameena had. He stood—while sitting, still too large, at this kitchen table—before a now-fallen soldier, in the shoes of a young, broken child. "That was the last thing he said. I saw it—I think I saw it, but…but I can't be sure. Nothing after those words seems real to me…he fell, forward and then down, onto his face, as if someone had pushed him from behind, and then—" "Please." Meir lifted his hand, moving it back and forth in dismissal. He knew he remembered this place—the cracked pathways, the dust that so easily kicked up into the air and yet fought back, surrounding and preventing the vision of those who had stepped on it, the white-bricked, red-roofed houses and the way they caved in when fired upon, the screams that could have been Khaled's. Meir now oddly felt as if he were holding a warm loaded gun, waving it before Jameena's face, threatening to shoot, rather than begging her to stop her story. But Jameena was grateful, because she didn't want to say anymore. She focused again on the stranger sitting before her—the stranger who was now softly touching the top of her hand—and thought she saw a tear on his cheek. He looked afraid, paralyzed, and his too-familiar eyes said, "Jameena, go home! Go home!" "Would you like to see him? Would you like to see Shav?" Meir broke an eternity's silence. He removed a small photograph from his coat chest-pocket. During gunfire exchanges, he was always positioned next to Shav. When Meir was blinded by dust and smoke, or when he couldn't move without risking getting shot by a hidden bullet, his only source of sanity was Shav, who would be lying there next to him, almost laughing to himself, and muttering, "This is fucked. This is all fucked." In the past months without Shav, this picture had served the same purpose. Jameena ran her fingers over the thinning photograph. "He looks like you." Meir didn't think so, but that didn't matter. "Khaled's killed a man before." Jameena spoke without awareness, as if the words that had left her mouth dictated her tongue and lips, and not the other way around. "He told me he'd never actually seen the bodies his bullets hit, but that he'd felt it. He said it were as if each bullet he shot were attached to his gun by a string, so that after striking flesh, it would send a numbing vibration first through his hand, then up to his elbow, and then to his chest." Meir knew this numbness too well. He nodded. "They hurt. The vibrations hurt." And as he said the words, he imagined both a bullet cutting through his chest, tearing flesh, and a forceful tingling beginning in his right fingertips, moving towards his wrist. He didn't know why, but he almost wanted to laugh. "I don't think you'll find Shav." "No, no I don't think I will."
