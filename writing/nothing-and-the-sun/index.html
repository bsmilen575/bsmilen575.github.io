Nothing and the Sun

2019

I got the call yesterday at 1:37 pm, and I know that for certain. I know the precise time of the call because I had been working—examining financial records and such—and I had just finished up and was going to go eat lunch, when suddenly it came. "Monsieur Alfred," the voice on the phone said, "you have been selected as a juror for the murder case of an Arab, murdered by a man named Meursault." I had no choice but to accept, so I did. If only I had gone to lunch a moment earlier...Perhaps if I had finished reviewing that painfully dull record just slightly faster, and then had gotten up and left my desk, I would have missed the call and the court would have passed over me, leaving me free to do my work and not preside over the murder of some Arab. But of course, it didn't happen like that. So, this morning I had to wake up quite some time before the sunrise (I wanted to get work done beforehand) and ride the bus down to the cour d'assises. Before I left, Julie told me not to be annoyed. She said most trials pass quickly, and that I'd soon be back at work. I agreed, but didn't tell her so, because I was tired and angry at the inconvenience this duty brought, and I wanted her continued sympathy. Instead, I told her I would pick up the bread on my way back from the courthouse. The ride didn't take too long, and it was pleasant because the bus was empty and so I could sleep. I knew it would be empty at this hour—the daily passengers don't usually enter until the 7:40 am stop, around 10 minutes after my usual entrance. There are always a few Arabs too (different ones each day), but they keep to themselves and don't take up much space, so other passengers and I don't mind them much. "Bonjour, Alfred! Going somewhere different today?" Cecile's loud voice—though preventing me from dozing off as I was just about to do—was welcoming to hear. He must have guessed that I wasn't going to work because it was much earlier than usual. "Yes, today I am going to the courthouse. I was called up yesterday and told that I've been selected as a juror, to hear the murder case of an Arab, murdered by a man named Meursault." Cecile loved to engage in conversation with his passengers. He knew a lot about me, and I knew even more about him (only because I've inferred much through my observations). For instance, I knew that he wasn't the always-jovial man he appeared to be, and that underneath all of his chatter lied the skeleton of a once-loved but now lonely man, who desperately tried to keep himself busy by talking. "To the Court we go then, Alfred! And remember—everyone's guilty!" … At the courthouse there were thirty-five others like myself, who had been called and told about the murder case. We were seated in a large, high-ceilinged room, at the front of which sat an urn filled with all of our names. We were told that each name would be read aloud, and when it was, a lawyer could object to it (if they didn't like the way it sounded, I suppose, because they knew nothing about the actual people behind the names) and then it would be discarded and the person to whom the name belonged would be free to leave. The arbitrators explained that the twelve names left would be the jurors of the case. I was excited to learn that I had a two in three chance of leaving and returning home and then to work. " 'Monsieur Bernard'… 'Objection'… 'Monsieur Jacques'… 'Objection'… 'Madame Clémence'… 'Objection'." I waited and waited for my name when finally it came: "Monsieur Alfred," and then…nothing. I was one of the remaining twelve. I sat there, along with the others, wondering confusedly what it was that made my name "special." Why were six letters, randomly sequenced to produce a certain sound, more appealing to these lawyers than other names? Would different lawyers have liked my name as well, or would they have rejected it? The whole process seemed strange to me, but before I had time to think too much about it, we were told that the trial would be beginning shortly, and that we must prepare ourselves to listen closely and think impartially. Some time passed—I'd venture a guess that it was only a matter of minutes or so—and then the courtroom began to fill. Reporters, journalists, policemen, and people whom I assumed to be spectators filed in noisily, causing much commotion. We were seated facing everyone, which was odd to me because this arrangement granted us (the ones with the most favorable and least threatening names) a sense of authority. And then, through all of the uproar, the accused man walked in. Immediately I felt that he was not entirely present. He walked slowly, escorted by two policemen, and keeping his gaze steady and locked straight ahead. I don't think he blinked once, and he seemed imperturbable—no, he seemed leisurely. I knew that he was a mad man—a sort of psychopath, maybe—and I knew this right away. I was certain. Then the man sat down and became obscured from my view. I stretched my neck and sat up straighter to see him, and noticed that the other jurors were doing the same. We stared and gawked at the man as if he were different from us, and not a man at all. I suddenly got the strange sensation that we were passengers on the morning bus, analyzing a new arrival—an interruption in our daily routines. It was as if we were searching him, trying to find something different or funny about him. But I rejected that absurd thought immediately. This man was not a new arrival on a bus—he was an accused murderer, and there was a grand difference between the two. I knew it. For a second—just a fleeting second—my eyes and the man's eyes locked. I felt as if my seat and his had been swapped, and now he was gawking at me, trying to find something funny. But soon I remembered where I was sitting. That was the first time that I'd felt fortunate to have been a juror. Soon I heard the bailiff's command to "All rise." Now, I thought that I'd listen to what this man, Meursault, had to say—maybe he wasn't mad, maybe he had a reason, some sort of explanation, or maybe he wasn't even guilty. I would just have to wait and see. The presiding judge announced that the calling of the witnesses was beginning, and then the bailiff read off some names: "Thomas Pérez, Raymond, Marie, Masson, Salamano." I watched these people—one old, sad looking man with paper skin, one beautiful young girl about my age, another old man (this one with darker skin and deeper eyes), and two seemingly tough guys—and wondered who they were. I hadn't even heard the case yet. But still, my ink-blot wheels were spinning. Finally, the judge went over the details: Meursault had been spending the day at a friend's house, hanging out on the beach and swimming in the water. Meursault and his two friends, Raymond and Masson, went for a walk and got into a fight with some Arabs (over matters having to do with Raymond and a girl). The now dead Arab injured Raymond, though not badly. Meursault confirmed that Raymond had been holding a gun, but that he took it from him. Why? That was never answered. Later, Meursault, in possession of this gun, walked on the beach directly to the Arabs, and shot one of them once, paused, and then fired four more bullets. I trusted that these details were true, because after reading each separate piece of information the judge asked Meursault, "Is that correct?" And each time, Meursault calmly replied, "Yes, Your Honor," as if confirming what he'd eaten for breakfast that morning. Meursault had killed the Arab—it was true. He fired one shot and then four more. But was he guilty? Surely a murder could be justified if one had a good enough reason for committing it. Factual actions meant nothing alone. To decide anything, I needed to know the intentions behind them. The judge carried on and said that now he must discuss a matter that may seem unnecessary to the case, but that may in fact end up being significant. He asked Meursault about his Maman's recent death. Suddenly Meursault's crime became more understandable—the man must have been in a state of despair after the passing of his Maman. The judge asked Meursault, "Was your Maman's death hard on you?" And, as if purposely to stop us jurors from forming any sort of opinion, Meursault responded: "Maman and I stopped expecting anything from each other, or from anyone else. We both got on with our new lives." It didn't sound like man was the slightest bit afflicted by the death of his Maman. The prosecutor (after getting the court's permission) then asked him if he intended to kill the Arab. "No," was all Meursault had to say. I couldn't make sense of any of it. I was typically good at thinking these types of things through, accurately perceiving people, and intuiting to come to well-reasoned conclusions. But for some reason this case was a black box. Meursault wasn't helping himself and he didn't seem to care. He didn't seem to care about anything. Did he not understand what was going on? Or was he entirely aware? The only thing I knew for certain was that I knew absolutely nothing. The first witnesses testified. It was revealed that Meursault wasn't even seen crying at his Maman's funeral. His lawyer pointed out however, that he also wasn't seen not crying. Later, he said towards the end of his defense: "Here we have a perfect reflection of this entire trial: everything is true and nothing is true!" I thought that statement seemed quite accurate. Though still, I couldn't get past Meursault's insensitivity towards his Maman's death. That part bothered me. In his remarks, the prosecutor emphasized Meursault's odd behavior, turning to us and saying, "The same man who the day after his mother died was seen at the cinema howling in laughter to La fille du puisatier killed a man without cause – with not even a morsel of reason. And admits to as much." That statement also seemed accurate. I got lost in my thoughts for a while, so I stopped listening. I assume most of the other jurors tuned out at some points as well, due to either boredom, confusion, or both—after all, a man can only listen and think for so long. I managed to hear a few words over my loud thoughts though, like "judgment" "monstrous" "callousness" and "soul." Things went on like this for a while, until finally it was announced that the trial would be reconvened in the afternoon, and we were released from our judgment seats. As I got up to have lunch I wondered some more about Meursault. I wondered what he was doing now, during these few hours. Perhaps he was planning his closing words—maybe he was perseverating over them; maybe he was preparing something that could entirely redeem him and bring him to salvation. Or maybe not. But my guess was as good as nothing. In the afternoon, the witnesses continued but really nothing about the case changed. We had all the facts, but no answers. The defense lawyer concluded with the statement: "I, too, have peered into this man's soul, but unlike the esteemed representative of the government prosecutor's office, I did see something there, and I can assure you that I read it like an open book." He spoke confidently but I knew even he didn't believe what he was saying. I felt oddly good when he spoke, because it was as if he were pleading to me, and in a way, this made me feel powerful. Then I remembered that it had only been my name that got me there. At last the judge asked Meursault if he had anything to add. Meursault asserted that he never intended to kill the Arab, and then strangely added: "it was because of the sun." That sounded like nonsense. The judge then told us where the deliberations would be held, and we all filed out of the courtroom. … The twelve of us sat around a table in a small, enclosed room with brightly painted white walls. We discussed Meursault's crime for only a few minutes, which surprised me. About 25 to be precise. No one seemed confused like I was. I gathered that people had already made up their minds, and that terrified me. I suddenly wanted more time, but the ballots were being handed out. I received mine and couldn't do anything but stare at it blankly. How could I decide a man's fate? A man named Meursault. Me, a man named Alfred. I looked up to see every other juror empty-handed and learned that they had all already handed in their ballots. Now I was alone, in that room full of deciders. And they stared at me—watched me intently. I felt as if I were being judged myself. For a while I stared back at them, trying to discover their decisions based on their eyes. This woman found Meursault guilty and was firm about it. That one, the one with the sensitive green eyes, was unsure…but eventually decided on innocent. This man thought Meursault was a monster from the beginning, and that one believed he was innocent by insanity. Six say guilty…one, two, three, four, five say innocent…could it be so? Could my vote be the determining one? I could no longer think. "Everyone's guilty! Everyone's guilty!" The phrase kept repeating in my head. I believed it. Beads of sweat were rolling down my forehead, covering my eyes in a thin, stinging film. I couldn't see anything now. But it was already done: guilty. … I don't know what exactly it was, but something compelled me to go watch the execution months later. That day, I felt that my body controlled my mind. My feet shuffled forward without my brain's permission—they brought me to the front of the crowd. The morning was cool, with some wind. Lots of people came to see the spectacle. I expected that the process of the execution would be long, but it wasn't. It was surprisingly efficient. The guilty Meursault was brought out and put into the correct position under the guillotine. Some words were uttered in the name of the French people. An excitement was surfacing somewhere within my chest. I had reached the very front. I was so close that I could just make out the guilty Meursault's face. I was numb. It hit me right then that he and I were one in the same. He caused a man to die, and so did I. "Everyone's guilty!" It happened quickly. The guillotine blade came down, and when it did, it caught the sun's glare and reflected it—concentrated it all into one, sharp blade that struck and blinded me momentarily. It suddenly occurred to me that I then understood what Meursault meant by "it was the sun." It was the sun and nothing all at once. None of it—none of this—mattered. Nothing could matter at all. When I regained my vision I saw a headless body lying where a man named Meursault had just been. I smiled, because I had the strange thought that that guillotine took two heads. I walked away to return home to Julie. And I decided to pick up some bread on the way there.
