Let’s talk about trash

2023–2024

A strategic proposal for space orbital debris cleanup

Today more than ever before, space around Earth is full of debris from defunct satellites, spent rocket stages, paint chips and metal fragments. Orbital debris threatens safety and jeopardizes the $1.3T space-to-earth opportunity.

How big of a problem is this?

Traveling at ~8 km/s (5 miles/second), a piece of debris the size of a fleck of paint can hit with the velocity of a bullet and damage or destroy spacecraft.

The growth of orbital debris (1957–2024)

Visualization is representative. Actual debris counts: NASA Orbital Debris Program Office, ESA Space Debris Office (2024).

Why is space debris inherently bad?

Collisions actively disrupt downstream space-to-earth economy ($1.3T by 2035), e.g., network connectivity via satellites, positioning and navigation services defense. They also threaten spaceflight safety

Crowding in orbit reduces positional opportunities for nation states

Risk of catastrophic effects rendering space unusable within the next 5-10 years

Opportunity cost of missing out on space debris itself as recyclable, circular space economy

Why is it getting worse?

Satellite launch into LEO (Low Earth Orbit) has increased 5x since 2019. Over the next 5 years, 70,000 LEO satellites are planned for launch

That would move us from ~11k satellites to 80k+ by 2030 (7x growth)

Satellite launch market forecasted to grow to $108B by 2035, from $15B

Market analysis & overview of today's tech

Governments, academia, and civil space actors try to solve space debris across three levers:

Policy

New deorbit rules are in effect by FCC, ESA, Space traffic coordination, EU SST, and U.S. Commerce Department

Passive mitigation

Majority of new space systems technology is being developed with deorbit sails, propulsion for EOL disposal, or metal docking plates for on-orbit servicing

Active remediation

Detecting and depositioning active debris. This is the only approach that reduces the ~200 million objects already in orbit

Capture bags are the most promising (lowest cost per removal) opportunity in large debris cleanup today. However, ground-based laser nudging is a clear strategic winner because <10cm debris objects are the biggest threat, least detectable, and least addressed.

Small debris: A strategic opportunity

Ground-based laser nudging is the first scalable, cost effective way to clean up the small debris swarm

Most threatening debris class

Untrackable in bulk, lethal at orbital speeds, causes majority of unexpected collision

No viable tech today

Ground-based laser nudging is an early-stage concept and first method with technical and economic viability at scale.

Leverages existing infrastructure

US government and a few startups already have detection radars, just need to close the loop between tracking and action

$1K–10K per object cost

Capex majority of cost (vs. $10M+ for large-object capture using robotics or capture bags) → one laser site can clear 1000s of debris objects / year (NASA estimate)

Open questions, considerations, next steps

Time to build

Likely 6 months to 1 year

Need to develop beyond SOTA lasers

16kJ, beam quality of 1, pulse width near 1ns

Strategic partnerships

Tech developer should form long term partnerships and licensing with United States Space Force (USSF) and Satellite Development Agency (SDA), and possibly the International Space Station (ISS)

Compounding moat

High today given focus on small debris only and highly scalable / cost effective. But limited compounding effects are foreseen today (e.g., no reusability/harvesting like capture bag approaches)
